Part One

Introduction

What breast cancer is, who it affects, and how to recognize it.

01
Introduction

What Is Breast Cancer?

An uncontrolled growth of abnormal cells in breast tissue — the most commonly diagnosed cancer worldwide.

Breast cancer begins when cells in the breast — most often in the milk ducts or lobules — start dividing abnormally. These cells can form a tumor and, if not caught early, invade surrounding tissue or spread to distant organs through the lymphatic system and bloodstream.

It is not one disease. There are multiple subtypes — each with different behavior, prognosis, and treatment. The receptor status of the tumor (ER, PR, HER2) is critical in guiding therapy.

While it mainly affects women, men account for roughly 1% of all cases. Age is the single largest risk factor; most diagnoses occur after age 50.

Introduction

Types & Risk Factors

Breast cancer has several subtypes, and risk is shaped by both genetics and lifestyle.

Common Types

Invasive Ductal Carcinoma (IDC)

The most common type (~80%). Starts in milk ducts, invades surrounding tissue.

Invasive Lobular Carcinoma (ILC)

Begins in milk-producing lobules. Often harder to detect on mammography.

Triple-Negative (TNBC)

Lacks ER, PR, and HER2 receptors. More aggressive; treated primarily with chemotherapy.

HER2-Positive

Overexpresses HER2 protein. Responds well to targeted therapies like trastuzumab.

Non-Modifiable

  • Female sex and increasing age
  • BRCA1 / BRCA2 gene mutations
  • Family history of breast or ovarian cancer
  • Dense breast tissue
  • Early menarche or late menopause

Modifiable

  • Physical inactivity and obesity
  • Alcohol consumption
  • Combined hormone replacement therapy
  • Smoking
  • Never breastfeeding
🎙
30-Second Script

"There are several types of breast cancer. The most common is invasive ductal carcinoma, accounting for about 80% of cases. Risk factors fall into two categories: those we cannot change — like our genes and family history — and those we can modify, such as physical activity, alcohol consumption, and body weight. Understanding these helps us identify who needs closer monitoring."

Introduction

Signs & Symptoms

Early breast cancer often causes no symptoms at all — which is exactly why scheduled screening matters so much.

🔵

Lump or Thickening

A new lump in the breast or underarm that feels different from surrounding tissue.

📐

Change in Shape or Size

Unexplained change in the contour or size of one breast.

🩹

Skin Changes

Dimpling, puckering, redness, or peau d'orange (orange-peel texture).

🔴

Nipple Changes

Inversion of a previously normal nipple or changes in areola appearance.

💧

Nipple Discharge

Discharge other than breast milk — especially if bloody or from one breast only.

🌡️

Warmth or Redness

Persistent warmth and redness may indicate inflammatory breast cancer.

🎙
30-Second Script

"The most recognized symptom of breast cancer is a new lump or thickening in the breast or underarm. However, breast cancer can also present as skin changes, nipple inversion, discharge, or unexplained changes in breast shape. Importantly, many early-stage cancers produce no symptoms at all — which is why regular screening with mammography is essential, especially after age 40."

Treatment

How breast cancer is staged and the main modalities used to treat it.

Treatment

Staging the Disease

Treatment decisions depend entirely on how far the cancer has spread. The TNM staging system is the global standard.

"Before treatment can begin, the cancer must be staged. Staging tells us how far the disease has spread — from Stage 0, which is non-invasive and nearly always curable, to Stage IV, which is metastatic. As this table shows, survival rates drop significantly with each stage. This reinforces how vital early detection is: a Stage I diagnosis carries a 99% five-year survival rate."

05
Treatment

Treatment Modalities

Most patients receive a combination of treatments tailored to their cancer's stage, subtype, and receptor status.

Surgery

Lumpectomy (breast-conserving) or mastectomy. Sentinel lymph node biopsy determines lymph node involvement. Reconstruction may follow mastectomy.

Radiation Therapy

High-energy beams destroy remaining cancer cells post-surgery. Standard after lumpectomy; reduces local recurrence risk by 50–70%.

Chemotherapy

Systemic drugs that kill rapidly dividing cells. Given before surgery (neoadjuvant) to shrink tumors, or after (adjuvant) to eliminate microscopic disease.

Hormone Therapy

For ER+/PR+ cancers. Tamoxifen or aromatase inhibitors block estrogen's growth-promoting effects. Typically taken for 5–10 years.

Targeted Therapy

Drugs targeting specific proteins — trastuzumab (Herceptin) for HER2+ cancers, CDK4/6 inhibitors (palbociclib) for advanced HR+ disease.

Immunotherapy

Checkpoint inhibitors such as pembrolizumab are now approved for high-risk triple-negative breast cancer in combination with chemotherapy.

🎙
30-Second Script

"Treatment for breast cancer is rarely a single approach. Surgery removes the primary tumor; radiation cleans up any remaining local disease; chemotherapy targets cancer cells throughout the body; hormone therapy blocks the estrogen that feeds many breast cancers; and targeted therapies like trastuzumab attack specific molecular features of the tumor. More recently, immunotherapy has been added for aggressive triple-negative cases."

Treatment

Side Effects & Survivorship

Treating the cancer is only part of the picture. Managing side effects and supporting long-term quality of life are equally important.

Surgery

  • Pain and swelling at incision site
  • Lymphedema if lymph nodes removed
  • Numbness or altered sensation
  • Body image and psychological impact

Chemotherapy

  • Fatigue and nausea
  • Hair loss (alopecia)
  • Increased infection risk (neutropenia)
  • Chemobrain (cognitive fog)

Radiation

  • Skin irritation and fatigue
  • Breast swelling or fibrosis
  • Rare: cardiac effects (left-sided radiation)
  • Fatigue lasting weeks after treatment

Hormone Therapy

  • Menopausal symptoms (hot flashes)
  • Joint pain and bone density loss
  • Mood changes
  • Increased blood clot risk (tamoxifen)

Survivorship care includes regular follow-up, monitoring for recurrence, bone density screening, psychological support, and healthy lifestyle counseling. Over 3.8 million breast cancer survivors are living in the US alone.

🎙
30-Second Script

"Every treatment modality comes with its own side-effect profile. Surgeons manage lymphedema; oncologists watch for neutropenia during chemotherapy; radiation can cause skin changes and fatigue. Hormone therapy, while essential for ER-positive cancers, can cause joint pain and bone loss. A good breast cancer care team addresses not just the tumor, but the whole patient's experience — including life after treatment."

Part Three

Genetics & BRCA Research

What we now know about inherited mutations and where the science is heading.

07
Research

The BRCA1 & BRCA2 Genes

These tumor suppressor genes normally protect us. When mutated, lifetime cancer risk rises dramatically.

BRCA1 — Chromosome 17

BRCA1 helps repair double-strand DNA breaks via homologous recombination. Loss-of-function mutations remove this repair checkpoint, allowing DNA damage to accumulate in breast and ovarian cells.

55–72% — Lifetime breast cancer risk

Also associated with up to 44% lifetime risk of ovarian cancer.

BRCA2 — Chromosome 13

BRCA2 also participates in homologous recombination repair. Mutations are associated with breast cancer in both women and men, as well as pancreatic and prostate cancers.

45–69% — Lifetime breast cancer risk

Male breast cancer risk: 6–8%. Also raises ovarian cancer risk to ~17%.

Inheritance Pattern

BRCA mutations follow an autosomal dominant pattern. A child of a carrier has a 50% chance of inheriting the mutation. Most carriers inherit the mutation from one parent.

Prevalence is approximately 1 in 400 in the general population, but 1 in 40 in those of Ashkenazi Jewish descent.

Testing & Counseling

Genetic testing is recommended for those with a strong family history — two or more first-degree relatives with breast/ovarian cancer, or any relative with a known BRCA mutation.

Pre- and post-test genetic counseling is essential to help patients understand results and implications for their family.

🎙
30-Second Script

"BRCA1 and BRCA2 are tumor suppressor genes that normally repair damaged DNA. When a person inherits a mutated copy, this repair mechanism fails, and breast and ovarian cells become vulnerable to malignant transformation. Carriers of BRCA1 mutations face a lifetime breast cancer risk of up to 72%, and BRCA2 carriers up to 69% — compared to around 12% in the general population. Identifying carriers early is life-saving."

08
Research

Key Discoveries in BRCA Research

From gene discovery to targeted therapy — three decades of breakthroughs.

1990

BRCA1 Locus Mapped

Mary-Claire King's team maps the BRCA1 gene locus to chromosome 17q21, the first evidence of a hereditary breast cancer gene.

1994–95

BRCA1 & BRCA2 Cloned

Both genes are isolated and sequenced, allowing the development of the first genetic tests for inherited breast cancer risk.

2013

Angelina Jolie & Public Awareness

After testing positive for BRCA1, Angelina Jolie publicly undergoes preventive mastectomy. Testing rates surge worldwide — the "Jolie Effect."

2014–18

PARP Inhibitors Approved

Olaparib and talazoparib are approved for BRCA-mutated metastatic breast cancer — the first targeted therapies that exploit the specific DNA repair defect caused by BRCA mutations.

2020s

Polygenic Risk Scores & Beyond BRCA

Research now incorporates hundreds of lower-risk variants into polygenic risk scores (PRS), enabling far more precise individual risk prediction beyond just BRCA1/2.

🎙
30-Second Script

"The story of BRCA research spans thirty years. It began with mapping the BRCA1 locus in 1990, followed quickly by sequencing both genes. Clinical testing followed. By 2013 public awareness surged dramatically. And critically, this research led directly to new therapies: PARP inhibitors, approved between 2014 and 2018, specifically target the DNA repair defect in BRCA-mutated tumors — turning a genetic vulnerability into a therapeutic target."

Research

Future Directions

Where BRCA and breast cancer genetics research is headed in the coming decade.

🧬

CRISPR Gene Correction

Experimental CRISPR-based approaches aim to correct BRCA mutations at the germline level. Still in early research, but could one day prevent hereditary breast cancer before it starts.

📊

Polygenic Risk Scores

Combining hundreds of common low-risk variants into a single score to predict individual lifetime risk more precisely — especially for those without BRCA mutations.

💊

Next-Generation PARP Inhibitors

New PARP inhibitors with better bioavailability and less toxicity are in trials. Combination with immunotherapy is being studied for triple-negative and BRCA-mutated cancers.

🩸

Liquid Biopsy

Detection of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) in blood can identify cancer earlier, monitor treatment response, and detect recurrence — all without invasive tissue biopsy.

🤖

AI-Assisted Screening

AI models trained on mammograms can now detect subtle abnormalities and flag high-risk patients for genetic counseling, augmenting radiologist performance.

🌍

Equity in Genetic Testing

Expanding access to BRCA testing in low- and middle-income countries — including Iran — is a global health priority, as most BRCA-related deaths occur in under-resourced settings.

🎙
30-Second Script

"The future of BRCA research is both exciting and urgent. CRISPR may one day allow us to correct mutations before cancer develops. Polygenic risk scores will make screening more personalized. Liquid biopsies can detect cancer through a blood draw. And ensuring that genetic testing reaches all populations — including in Iran and across the developing world — is perhaps the most important challenge of all. The science is advancing; access must keep pace."
